Police Complaints Authority (India)

Police Complaints Authorities (PCAs) are statutory or executive bodies in Indian States and Union Territories that examine complaints from the public about police misconduct. Typical complaints include poor or improper investigation, refusal to register an FIR, custodial torture and high-handed behaviour by police officers. The authorities make recommendations for disciplinary action to the State government, which holds the power to act on them.

Origins

The authorities were mandated by the Supreme Court's 2006 judgment in Prakash Singh v. Union of India, as one of seven police reform directives, in response to the large volume of complaints against the police and the lack of accountability. Seventeen States set up PCAs through State Police Acts, and ten States did so through executive orders.

District and State authorities

A District Police Complaints Authority usually has a chairperson, often a retired district judge, and one or two members. It handles complaints against officers below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police. Complaints against officers of higher rank go to the State Police Complaints Authority.

How to complain to a PCA

  1. Identify the rank of the officer: below Deputy SP goes to the district authority; SP and above goes to the State authority.
  2. Submit a written complaint with your details, the officer's name or badge number, date, place and a description of the incident, along with any evidence such as medical reports or photographs.
  3. The authority may call for a report from the police, hear the parties, and then recommend departmental or criminal action.

Criticism

The authorities have been described as "lame-duck" institutions: their powers are often diluted, proceedings are slow, many lack independent investigators, and they have had limited effect on police culture.
